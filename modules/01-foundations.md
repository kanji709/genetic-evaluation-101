# Module 01 — Foundations of quantitative genetics

## Learning objectives

- Decompose a phenotype into genetic and environmental parts
- Define additive genetic value (breeding value) and explain why it is what we select on
- Define and interpret heritability
- Explain why genetic evaluation is needed rather than selecting on raw phenotype

## Core concepts

**Phenotype model.** An observed trait value is

  P = G + E,  and  G = A + D + I

where A is the additive (breeding) value, D dominance and I epistasis. Only **A** is reliably passed from parent to offspring, because parents transmit alleles, not genotypes.

**Breeding value.** The value of an individual as a parent: twice the expected deviation of its offspring's mean from the population mean.

**Variance partition.** σ²P = σ²A + σ²D + σ²I + σ²E

**Narrow-sense heritability.** h² = σ²A / σ²P — the proportion of phenotypic variance due to additive effects. It also equals the regression of breeding value on phenotype.

**Why evaluate?** Phenotypes are confounded by environment (herd, year, management) and individuals have different amounts of information. Genetic evaluation separates these effects and combines information from relatives.

## Worked example

A ram has a fleece weight 0.8 kg above its contemporary group mean. If h² = 0.35, its predicted breeding value from own record alone is

  EBV = h² × (P − P̄) = 0.35 × 0.8 = 0.28 kg

## Exercises

1. Two animals have the same phenotype but h² differs between traits (0.1 vs 0.5). Which EBV is more trustworthy, and why?
2. Why is dominance variance not useful for predicting offspring performance?
3. Give two examples of environmental effects you would need to adjust for in a dairy herd.
